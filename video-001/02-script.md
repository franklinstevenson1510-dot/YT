# Video 001 — "He Sold the Eiffel Tower. Twice."
Target runtime: ~10–11 min | Narrator: calm, dry, quietly ominous
(Facts marked ⚠ are retold in legend/memoir and disputed — keep the hedging language.)

---

## COLD OPEN (0:00–0:25)
[VISUAL: black screen, faint city ambience. Slow push-in on archival Eiffel Tower photo, 1920s]
[SFX/MUSIC: low drone begins]

In 1925, a man sat in a Paris hotel suite and sold the Eiffel Tower.
Not a postcard. Not a model. The tower itself, three hundred meters of iron, to a man who handed over a briefcase of cash.
And the part nobody believes: the buyer never went to the police.
Because the con wasn't the lie. The con was what the lie let the victim do to himself.
[VISUAL: red text slams in: "VICTOR LUSTIG"]
Stay with me, because this isn't even his best story. His best story involves Al Capone.

## WHO IS THIS GUY (0:25–1:30)
[VISUAL: map of Europe, Austria-Hungary borders, pin on Hostinné; sepia photos]

Victor Lustig was born in 1890 in a small town in what is now the Czech Republic. By his twenties he spoke several languages, and he had a talent that looks harmless on paper: people trusted him within about ninety seconds.
He wasn't a thief in the ordinary sense. He didn't pick locks. He didn't break in anywhere.
He waited to be invited.
[VISUAL: ocean liner, 1910s–20s]
After the First World War, he worked the transatlantic liners, targeting rich travelers with time to kill. He had polished suits, a quiet manner, and a rule he'd later write down himself: never look bored.
Paris, 1925, is where all of it comes together.

## THE SETUP (1:30–3:15)
[VISUAL: 1925 newspaper layout (recreated), Eiffel Tower under scaffolding]
[SFX/MUSIC: tick of a clock, tension builds]

Here's the situation. The Eiffel Tower was built for the 1889 World's Fair, and it was never meant to last. Its permit was for twenty years. By 1925 it was expensive, rusting, and in need of constant repainting.
Lustig reads a newspaper article about the cost of maintaining it. And an idea forms.
What if the government quietly decided to get rid of it?
[VISUAL: forged letterhead being typed, Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs]
He forges official government stationery. He writes to six scrap-metal dealers, inviting them to a confidential meeting at the Hôtel de Crillon, one of the most luxurious hotels in Paris.
At the meeting, he introduces himself as a deputy director-general of the Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs.
And he tells them the story. The tower is a financial disaster. The government wants it gone. But if the public learns the plan, there will be outrage, so it has to be handled quietly. Sealed bids. Total secrecy.
Six men leave that room thinking they've been handed the deal of the century.
Only one of them will make Lustig rich. His name is André Poisson.

## ESCALATION 1: THE CHOSEN MARK (3:15–5:00)
[VISUAL: portrait silhouette labeled "POISSON", others fading out]

Lustig spent a few days studying the six dealers. Poisson stood out. He was successful, but he was new to Paris's inner business circles, and he wanted in. His wife had doubts. Poisson did not want to be the man who hesitated.
[VISUAL: Lustig and Poisson in a car, motion blur of Paris]
Lustig took Poisson on a drive. He hinted that civil servants aren't paid well. That his position was difficult. That a man in his situation might expect a little something to keep the paperwork moving.
And Poisson, eager to prove he understood how the world worked, offered him a bribe.
[SFX: pause. silence.]
That's the whole trick. Lustig never asked for it. He let Poisson buy the tower AND bribe the official. Poisson paid for both.
A few days later, Lustig was on a train to Vienna with a case full of cash.

## THE TWIST (5:00–6:30)
[VISUAL: newspapers on a table, nobody reading them]

Now, he waited for the headline. A scandal. Police. An arrest warrant.
Nothing.
Poisson was a man who had just bribed a government official and bought a national monument. Going to the police meant admitting he'd been a fool, and admitting he'd tried to buy his way into a crooked deal. He stayed silent.
Lustig read the Paris papers for weeks. Nothing.
So he went back to Paris and did it all again with a new set of six dealers.
This time one of them went to the police. Lustig escaped to the United States before anyone could catch him. ⚠ (Details of the second attempt vary by source.)

## ESCALATION 2: THE BOX (6:30–8:00)
[VISUAL: a mahogany box, brass dials, patent-style diagrams]

In America he took the idea further. He built a small wooden box with rollers and dials and called it the Romanian Box. Insert a real $100 bill, wait six hours, and a perfect copy emerges.
He'd demonstrate it twice. Two perfect bills. The mark would buy the box, often for thousands of dollars, and by the time they discovered it only copied if you loaded real notes in, Lustig was gone.
He sold it more than once. The price reportedly reached tens of thousands of dollars.

## ESCALATION 3: CAPONE (8:00–9:15)
[VISUAL: 1920s Chicago street, Capone archival portrait]
[SFX/MUSIC: low double-bass, threat]

And then he met Al Capone. ⚠ This part comes mostly from Lustig's own legend.
The story goes: Lustig asked Capone for a $50,000 investment in a scheme. He promised to double it in sixty days. Capone, who did not lend money to strangers, handed it over, with a warning of what would happen if it went wrong.
Lustig put the cash in a safe-deposit box and did nothing. Two months later he returned to Capone, apologetic: the plan had failed. Here's your $50,000 back.
Capone, so impressed by the honesty, handed him $5,000.
Lustig had never planned to invest. He just wanted a loan he could hold for a few weeks, and he left with a gift.

## THE FALL (9:15–10:15)
[VISUAL: federal agents, 1935 headline "COUNTERFEITER CAUGHT"; Alcatraz exterior]

His mistake was the one con men always make. He got greedy.
By the 1930s he was running a counterfeiting operation in New York. In 1935 the U.S. Secret Service arrested him. Weeks later he escaped from federal detention by hanging a rope of bedsheets out the window, pretending to be a window washer in front of a crowd. It took the Secret Service about a month to find him.
He was sentenced to 20 years and sent to Alcatraz.
In 1947 he died of pneumonia in a federal prison hospital. On his death certificate, under occupation, it said: "salesman."

## THE TELL (10:15–11:00)
[VISUAL: ten rules typed on paper, one highlighted in red]
[SFX/MUSIC: music resolves]

Lustig left behind a list of rules. "Be a patient listener." "Never look bored." "Never boast, let your importance be quietly obvious."
But the real lesson isn't on his list.
The Tell is this: **the secrecy was the con**. "Don't tell anyone" is the sentence that has been in every scam since. If the deal only works if nobody checks it, it isn't a deal.
